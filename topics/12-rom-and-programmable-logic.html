Before we start

Why this matters

How did a 1980s arcade machine know exactly which pixel-art villain to throw at you next, with zero software as we'd recognise it today?

It was hard-wired straight into a ROM chip — a lookup table baked into silicon that will never, ever change its mind. This chapter shows you that a ROM is basically a truth table you can buy off a shelf, and how PLAs and PALs let you “program” the wiring yourself instead of begging a chip factory for a custom favour.

Concept 1 of 4

ROM organisation and logic

A 2n × m ROM has n address lines feeding a decoder, and the decoder’s word lines feed OR gates that form the m output bits. A 32 × 8 ROM has 5 inputs and 8 outputs. Reading applies an address, decodes it, selects one word and outputs its bits.

Because the decoder generates every minterm, a ROM implements any combinational function of its address bits. The inputs are the address, and the stored bits are the truth table. A full adder (3 inputs, 2 outputs) fits in an 8 × 2 ROM.

Concept 3 of 4

PLA and PAL

A PLA has a programmable AND array that forms product terms and a programmable OR array that combines them, so it implements sum-of-products directly with only the terms needed. A PAL has a programmable AND array and a fixed (or constrained) OR array: cheaper and faster, less flexible.

Concept 4 of 4

Design flow

  1. Define the functions.
  2. Simplify the Boolean expressions (don’t-cares help).
  3. Identify product terms, sharing them across outputs.
  4. Map terms to the device.
  5. Program and verify.

Modern programmable logic includes CPLDs and FPGAs, which scale the same idea to thousands of configurable blocks.

Summary

Summary

  • A ROM is a lookup table: decoder plus OR array.
  • Mask, PROM, EPROM, EEPROM and Flash differ in how they are programmed and erased.
  • PLA: AND and OR both programmable. PAL: AND programmable, OR fixed.
  • Share product terms to save resources.
  • CPLDs and FPGAs are the modern versions.
